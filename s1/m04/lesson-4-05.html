★ A leer — Ana y su familia en Guatemala
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
No new grammar today — just reading. You'll meet Ana and hear tener, ages, and possessives working together in a real little story.
This is your third encounter with tener — and the most relaxed. Instead of choosing forms in a drill, you'll simply notice them at work inside meaningful input: a short paragraph about a girl and her family in Guatemala. Read it, listen to it, and let the pattern settle. Then we'll take a cultural detour to Guatemala, where Ana is from.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read and understand a short paragraph that uses tener, ages, and possessives.
2
I will be able to locate Guatemala and name one thing it is known for.
⏰ PART 1 · ANTES DE LEER 8 min

Before you read

Good readers guess before they read. Ana is a teenager in Guatemala with a big household — parents, siblings, and a grandmother who lives with them. As you go into the paragraph, keep one question in mind: how many people are in her family, and how old is each one?

💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · no wrong answer
In your home, does anyone besides your parents live with you — a grandparent, a cousin, an aunt or uncle?
📚 PART 2 · LA LECTURA · ANA 16 min

Listen first, then read

Click 🔊 to hear the whole paragraph read aloud by a native speaker. Listen once with your eyes closed, then read along a second time. Don't worry about every word — you already know the important ones.

Escuchar la lectura completa — Ana

«¡Hola! Me llamo Ana y soy de Guatemala. Mi familia es grande. Tengo dos hermanos y una hermana. Mi hermano mayor tiene dieciocho años y mi hermana menor tiene ocho años. Mi abuela vive con nosotros; ella tiene setenta años. También tenemos un primo en la ciudad de Antigua. Hoy tengo hambre porque es la hora del almuerzo. ¡Mi familia y yo estamos muy contentos!»

Not sure of a word? Reveal a mini-glossary — but try to read without it first.

me llamo = my name is · grande = big · mayor = older · menor = younger · vive con nosotros = lives with us · también = also · la hora del almuerzo = lunchtime · porque = because · hambre = hunger · contentos = happy
✅ PART 3 · ¿COMPRENDISTE? 16 min

Check your understanding

Answer from the paragraph. These are reading questions — you're showing you understood.

✅ TRY IT · from the reading
¿De dónde es Ana? (Where is Ana from?)
✅ TRY IT · from the reading
¿Cuántos años tiene el hermano mayor de Ana? (How old is Ana's older brother?)
✅ TRY IT · from the reading
¿Quién vive con la familia de Ana? (Who lives with Ana's family?)

In your own words: why does Ana say tengo hambre and not estoy hambre or soy hambre? Say it out loud, then reveal.

Because Spanish treats hunger as something you have, not something you are. Tener hambre = to be hungry. It's the same “have,”-not-“be” pattern as age (tengo ocho años). Never estoy or soy for hunger.
⭐ PARA IR MÁS LEJOS · optional · +5 min
Go back to the paragraph and be a detective. Find every form of tener (there are five: tengo, tiene, tiene, tenemos, tengo) and every possessive (mi). Ana also uses soy, es, and estamos — can you say why each one? (Origin/identity → ser; feeling now → estar; having/age → tener.) This mix of three verbs is exactly what Day 10 will ask for.
🌎 CULTURA · GUATEMALA
Un país de colores y volcanes

Ana is from Guatemala, a country in Central America just south of Mexico. Its capital is la Ciudad de Guatemala, and its old colonial capital, Antigua — where Ana's cousin lives — sits among green volcanoes and cobblestone streets.

Guatemala is the heart of the living Maya world: more than twenty Maya languages are spoken there, and families are famous for their brilliantly colored, hand-woven huipiles (traditional blouses), each pattern tied to a specific town. The marimba, a wooden xylophone, is the national instrument — you'll hear it at family celebrations across the country.

★ LA VIRTUD · RESPONSIBILITY

Notice how Ana's household shares life across three generations — her grandmother vive con nosotros, and everyone looks after everyone. In many Guatemalan families, caring for los abuelos at home is a quiet, daily responsibility that children learn young. A family, like a woven huipil, holds together when every thread does its part.
