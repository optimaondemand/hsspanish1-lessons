★ Marta en Sevilla — A Reading & a Taste of Spain
Lesson · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Your grammar meets a real person — Marta, who shops and cooks in Sevilla.
This is your third encounter with saber/conocer and the pronouns — but instead of drills, they live inside a short story. You'll read (and hear) Marta describe her neighborhood market and her Sunday tortilla española, spotting each grammar move as it happens. Then a deeper look at Spanish food culture. Nothing new to memorize — just the tools you have, working in the wild.

1
I will be able to read a short passage and find saber, conocer, and direct-object pronouns at work.
2
I will be able to describe a few features of Spanish food culture.

📖 PART 2 · A LEER 13 min

Marta, de Sevilla — listen and read

Press play and follow along. Then read it once more on your own, watching for the bold grammar: a conocer, a saber, and the pronoun la that keeps standing in for the tortilla.

Play the reading

«Me llamo Marta y vivo en Sevilla, España. Conozco un mercado muy bueno cerca de mi casa. Allí compro fruta, pescado y queso. Sé preparar una tortilla española deliciosa. ¿La quieres probar? La hago los domingos para mi familia.»

⭐ COMPRUEBA · optional
Point to each bold word and say its job: Conozco (a place she's familiar with), Sé (a skill — how to make the tortilla), La quieres probar / La hago (the pronoun la replacing la tortilla so she doesn't repeat it). Why la and not lo? Because tortilla is feminine.

✅ READING CHECK · the pronoun
In «¿La quieres probar?», what does la stand for?

Your turn: answer Marta's question — «¿La quieres probar?» — using a pronoun (“Yes, I want to try it”). Say it, then reveal.

Sí, la quiero probar. (Or Sí, quiero probarla.) Both are correct — la replaces la tortilla, before the verb or attached to the infinitive.
🌎 CULTURA · ESPAÑA
El domingo en familia

Marta makes her tortilla los domingos para mi familia — and that's very Spanish. In Spain, la comida del domingo (Sunday lunch) is often the biggest, slowest meal of the week, when several generations gather around one table. The meal itself is usually eaten in the early afternoon, around 2 or 3 p.m., later than in the United States. Food, in Spain, is time spent together.

🍣 CULTURA A FONDO · ESPAÑA
Los mercados de abastos

Long before supermarkets, Spanish cities were fed by mercados de abastos — covered public markets full of stalls (puestos) selling fresh pescado, carne, queso, fruta, and verduras. Many are over a century old and still busy every morning. Sevilla's Mercado de Triana and Madrid's famous Mercado de San Miguel are two you might visit one day.

Shopping there is a conversation, not a checkout line: you greet the vendor, ask what's fresh, sometimes pruebas a sample, and pay la cuenta with a «gracias, hasta luego.» Increasingly these markets also hold small tapas bars, so you can shop and eat — a very Spanish way to spend a morning.
