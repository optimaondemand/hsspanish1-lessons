Lesson 6.08
★ Fonética — The Accent That Makes the Past
Lesson + graded oral task · about 40 minutes
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You've written the preterite all week. Today you say it — and you record it.
This is a pronunciation clinic. In the preterite, one sound does most of the work: the stressed final vowel in the yo and él/ella forms — hablÉ, comÍ, comiÓ. Say it in the wrong place and “I spoke” (hablé) turns into “I speak” (hablo). We'll rehearse that stress out loud, then you'll record your first graded Tarea oral: three true sentences about what you did last weekend.

⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 5 min

Why the accent matters so much

In Spanish, an accent mark tells you exactly where to punch a word. In the preterite that punch is the whole difference between present and past: HA-blo (I speak) vs. ha-BLÉ (I spoke). Get the stress right and your listener instantly knows you mean the past. Today we lock in that habit, then you use it on air.

📚 PART 2 · EL ACENTO DEL PRETÉRITO 11 min

Punch the last vowel

Three preterite endings carry a written accent, and it always pulls the stress to the very end of the word. Say each one slowly, exaggerating the punch:

EndingWhere the stress landsContrast with present
-é (yo, -ar)habl-É, camin-Évs. HA-blo, ca-MI-no
-í (yo, -er/-ir)com-Í, escrib-Ívs. CO-mo, es-CRI-bo
-ió (él/ella)com-IÓ, escrib-IÓvs. CO-me, es-CRI-be

Model verbs — listen, then echo

Click 🔊, then say each back, landing the punch on the final vowel.

  • hablé«Ayer hablé…» — punch on -BLÉ
  • comió«Ella comió…» — punch on -MIÓ
  • caminé, comí, escribíthree endings in a row — all end-stressed
⭐ PARA IR MÁS LEJOS · optional · +5 min
Record yourself on your phone saying «hablo / hablé» and «como / comí» back to back, then play it back. Can you hear the stress jump to the end in the past form? That playback is the fastest way to fix your own accent.
🧠 PART 3 · LAS FORMAS SIN ACENTO 9 min

The other forms follow the default rule

Not every preterite form has a written accent. Hablaste, hablamos, hablaron, comiste, comimos, comieron follow the normal rule you already know: a word ending in a vowel, -n, or -s is stressed on the second-to-last syllable. So:

  • ha-BLAS-te, ha-BLA-mos, ha-BLA-ron — no accent mark needed.
  • co-MIS-te, co-MI-mos, co-MIE-ron — same default.

Where does the stress go in bailaron? Say it out loud, then reveal.

bai-LA-ron — ends in -n, so the default puts the stress on the second-to-last syllable. No accent mark.

And in viajé? Say it, then reveal.

via-JÉ — the written accent overrides the default and punches the final é. That accent is what marks it as past.
🌎 ESPAÑA A FONDO · CÓMO SUENA
El ceceo: una «z» con historia

In much of Spain, the letters z and soft c are pronounced like the English th in “think” — so gracias sounds like “GRA-thias.” This is called ceceo/distinción. In Latin America (and our course register), those same letters sound like s — that's seseo. Neither is “more correct”; they're regional. Our audio uses the Latin-American s, so visité keeps a clean s sound. If you travel to Madrid, you'll hear the th everywhere — now you'll know why.
