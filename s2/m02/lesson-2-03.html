TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you saw the vowel change. Today you learn the whole rule — including the one form that refuses to change.
These are stem-changing verbs (verbos de cambio radical). There are four common patterns — e→ie, o→ue, u→ue, and e→i — and one rule that ties them all together: the stem changes in every form except nosotros. Because the changing forms wrap around the table like a boot, teachers call these “boot verbs.” Master this and you unlock some of the most useful verbs in Spanish: querer (to want), poder (to be able to), dormir (to sleep), pedir (to ask for), and jugar (to play). To finish, a short ear-quiz pulls the rooms, furniture, and chore words from Days 1 and 2 back out of your memory.

⚔Para empezar · Los molinos de viento8 min

Giant or windmill? Look closely before you charge

In 1605 Miguel de Cervantes published Don Quijote de la Mancha. Its hero, a gentleman of about fifty who has read too many books about knights, rides out in his great-grandfather’s old armour on a thin old horse, Rocinante, with a farmer, Sancho Panza, as his squire.

On the plain of La Mancha he sees thirty or forty windmills and is certain they are giants. Sancho warns him: “no son gigantes, sino molinos de viento” (they are not giants, but windmills). Don Quijote charges anyway. A sail catches his lance and throws him and Rocinante across the field. He saw an enemy that was not there, and his mistake cost him dearly.

A wrong verb form can fool you the same way: quero looks almost like quiero. Each shape on the plain carries a banner with a verb form. Wrong form? It is only a windmill: turn away, and Don Quijote earns a new, shinier piece of armour. Right form? It is a real giant: charge!

One thing to hold onto

Everything today rests on a single idea: the ending is normal; the stem vowel is what changes. You already conjugate regular verbs perfectly — -o, -es, -e, -emos, -en. Keep those endings; just watch the vowel inside.

IIILA BOTA · EL VERBO COMPLETO8 min

Where the change happens — and where it doesn't

Here is querer (ie) in full again, now that you know all four patterns. The stem changes in every form except nosotros. Those changed forms are shaded — they draw the shape of a boot. The same boot fits every pattern: poder (ue), jugar (ue), pedir (i).

Singularone personPluralmore than one
1st personI / weyoquiero nosotros/as · outside the bootqueremos
2nd personyoutúquieres —
3rd personhe, she, they; usted(es)usted / él / ellaquiere ustedes / ellos / ellasquieren

shaded = inside the boot: the stem changes (e→ie). Dashed = nosotros, outside the boot: no change.

Hear the exception out loud: Nosotros queremos ordenar la sala. — queremos, not “quieremos.” The nosotros form always keeps the original infinitive stem: dormir → dormimos, poder → podemos, jugar → jugamos, pedir → pedimos.

💡 THE MEMORY HOOK

Picture a boot drawn around the five forms. Everything inside the boot changes; nosotros sits outside the boot — so it stays regular. If you can spot “we,” you know the vowel does not change.

🌎 CULTURA · SANTIAGO Y LOS ANDES
Una casa con vista a la cordillera

From many windows in Santiago you can see the cordillera de los Andes rising right behind the city. On a clear winter morning the peaks are white with snow — and remember, in Chile winter falls in June, July, and August, because it's in the Southern Hemisphere. Muchos santiaguinos quieren una casa con vista a la montaña — many people in Santiago want a home with a mountain view (there's your quieren).

🏔 CULTURA A FONDO · CHILE
Las casas de Pablo Neruda

Chile's most famous poet, Pablo Neruda (winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature), loved his houses so much that they are museums today. La Chascona sits on a hillside in Santiago; La Sebastiana overlooks the port of Valparaíso; and his beloved home at Isla Negra faces the Pacific, filled with ships in bottles and sea shells. Neruda designed rooms like a poem — proof that la casa can be one of the most personal things a person builds.
