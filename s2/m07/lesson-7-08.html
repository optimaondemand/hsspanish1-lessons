⏰ PART 1 · PARA EMPEZAR 5 min

Why these sounds matter

Travel words are full of sounds that trip up English speakers: the h you must not say, the -ión that pulls the stress to the end, and preterite verbs whose stress sits in a surprising place. Lock these in and your trip story will sound smooth on the recording.

🔈 PART 2 · LA H MUDA (SILENT H) 9 min

The letter you never say

In Spanish the h is always silent. hotel sounds like “o-TEL,” hice like “I-se,” hacer like “a-SER.” Don't breathe on it at all. Listen and echo:

  • el hotelno h sound → “o-TEL”
  • hice“I-se” — the h is invisible to the ear

Bonus: the same silent-h rule makes hacer, hice, and hicieron all start with a clean vowel sound. Write the h, but never say it.

🧠 PART 3 · EL ACENTO EN -IÓN 9 min

Words that punch the last syllable

Words ending in -ión carry a written accent that lands the stress firmly on that last syllable — and the ió glides together as one quick sound. Say them big at the end:

  • avión → a-vi-ÓN
  • excursión → ex-cur-si-ÓN
  • estación (station) → es-ta-ci-ÓN
  • el aviónstress on -ÓN
  • la excursiónstress on -ÓN

Where does the stress fall in excursión, and why? Decide, then reveal.

On the last syllable: ex-cur-si-ÓN. The written accent on the ó overrides the default and pulls the stress to the end.
🧠 PART 4 · EL PRETÉRITO FUERTE (STRONG PRETERITE) 7 min

No accent → stress the stem

Here's a subtle one. Regular preterites stress (and accent) the last syllable: via-JÉ, ex-plo-RÉ. But today's irregular ones carry no accent and stress the stem instead: HI-ce, TU-ve, es-TU-ve. Compare and hear the difference:

  • hicestress on HI-, no accent (vs. regular via-JÉ)
  • tuvestress on TU-, no accent
  • estuvestress on -TU-, no accent

This is why there's no accent mark: the stress already sits where the default rule puts it (second-to-last syllable). Say them “pulled back,” not pushed to the end.

🌎 CULTURA · A FONDO · EL QUECHUA EN EL ESPAÑOL
Palabras que viajaron

Many everyday Spanish (and English) words came from quechua, the language of the Andes: papa (potato), cancha (field/court), carpa (tent), poncho, and cóndor. Notice these are easy to pronounce with pure Spanish vowels — no silent letters, no glides. When you speak them, you're using words that traveled from the mountains Andrea visited into languages all over the world.

🎤 PART 5 · TAREA ORAL 10 min

Your graded recording (15 points)

Record yourself telling a short story about a trip — real or imagined — in the past tense. Say where you went, what you did, and where you were. Use at least three preterite verbs, and include at least one irregular (fui, hice, tuve, or estuve). Aim for 30–45 seconds.

📝 A MODEL (yours can be about any trip)

«El verano pasado fui a Perú con mi familia. Hice una excursión a Machu Picchu con un guía. Estuve en las montañas una semana y tuve una aventura increíble.»
(Four irregulars — fui, hice, estuve, tuve — and no accents on any of them.)

Need to hear the target verbs one more time before you record? Replay these:

  • Ayer fui a Machu Picchu.ir — I went
  • Fui…, hice…, estuve…three irregulars, one breath
