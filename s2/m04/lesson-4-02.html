TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday you learned the actions. Today you learn how to order them — and the little word that turns each verb on.
First you'll pick up seven routine and sequence words (la rutina (the routine), temprano (early), tarde (late), primero (first), antes (before), después (after), el despertador (the alarm clock)) so you can string actions into an order. IIIEL PRONOMBRE REFLEXIVO17 min

Why does Spanish need the -se?

In Spanish, many verbs can do an action to something else or to yourself. The pronoun (me, te, se, nos) is how Spanish says the action lands back on the person doing it. Four things make it click.

1. You have said one since your first week. Me llamo Camila. We translate it as “My name is Camila,” but word for word it says “I call myself Camila.” Llamar is “to call”; me is “myself.” ¿Cómo te llamas? is “What do you call yourself?” You were already using a reflexive verb.

2. The same verb, with and without the pronoun. Without me, you do the action to something else. With me, you do it to yourself, or to part of yourself.

SpanishWhat it means
Levanto la mano.I raise my hand. (the hand goes up)
Me levanto.I get up. Word for word: “I raise myself.” (you go up)
Lavo la mesa.I wash the table. (the table gets washed)
Me lavo la cara.I wash my face. (your own face gets washed); me shows it is yours

3. English often hides the “myself.” With these verbs, Spanish says it. In English we say “I get up,” “I get dressed,” “I wake up” and leave out who the action lands on. With these routine verbs, Spanish keeps the pronoun:

  • me levanto — I get up (I raise myself)
  • me visto — I get dressed (I dress myself)
  • me despierto — I wake up (I wake myself)

So when English has no “myself,” don’t drop the pronoun in Spanish. Levanto alone means you are lifting something.

4. Whose face? La, not mi. Me lavo la cara, not mi cara. Me cepillo los dientes, not mis dientes. Levanto la mano, not mi mano. When it is clear whose body it is, Spanish uses el, la, los, las for parts of the body, where English uses “my.” In me lavo la cara, the me makes it clear the face is yours. In levanto la mano there is no pronoun, but it is still clear: in class, you raise your own hand.

The little word that makes it yours

A reflexive verb has two parts: a pronoun that says who the action lands on, and the verb conjugated the normal way. Drop the -se, pick the matching pronoun, and put it in front. Here is levantarse (to get up), a nice regular -ar verb. Click each 🔊 and repeat.

SubjectPronounlevantarseListen & repeat
yomeme levanto Yo me levanto a las seis. (I get up at six.)
tútete levantas ¿A qué hora te levantas? (What time do you get up?)
usted / él / ellasese levanta Ella se levanta tarde. (She gets up late.)
nosotros/asnosnos levantamos Nosotros nos duchamos por la mañana. (We shower in the morning.)
ustedes / ellos / ellassese levantan Ellos se acuestan tarde. (They go to bed late.) Preview: acostarse is one o→ue stem-changer, like those in Módulo 2 — the full story is tomorrow.

(The verb endings are the same regular endings you already know — the only new piece is the pronoun in front. Notice usted/él/ella and ustedes/ellos/ellas both use se. In Latin America, ustedes covers "you all" in every setting; vosotros, Spain's informal "you all," you'll meet later, labeled as European.)

🌎 CULTURA · EL RITMO DEL DÍA
La sobremesa

In Ecuador and across Latin America, the daily routine often includes something English has no word for: the sobremesa — the unhurried time spent at the table talking after a meal is finished. Lunch (el almuerzo) is frequently the biggest meal of the day, and families linger. It's a small reminder that a "routine" isn't only about rushing through tasks — it also makes room to sit down (sentarse) and be together.

🌎 CULTURA A FONDO · ECUADOR
El dólar y el kichwa

Two everyday facts surprise many learners: since 2000, Ecuador's official currency has been the US dollar — so a student in Quito pays for breakfast in the same dollars used in the United States. And alongside Spanish, many Ecuadorians speak Kichwa, an Indigenous Andean language; words like ñaño (brother/buddy) slip into everyday Ecuadorian Spanish. A daily routine can be lived in more than one language.
