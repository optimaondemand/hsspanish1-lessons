📚 PART 2 · LA C Y LA Z 10 min

The soft c and z = “s” (in Latin America)

Across Latin America, c before e or i, and the letter z, are pronounced like an English s. (Before a, o, u, the c is a hard k: cumbia, concierto.)

SpellingSoundExample
ce / ci“s”canción, celebración, concierto
z“s”disfraz, ritmo (no z here — compare to tiza)
ca / co / cuhard “k”cumbia, concierto, casa

Listen and echo — watch the soft c:

  • la cancióncan-si-ón — soft c
  • la celebraciónse-le-bra-si-ón — two soft c's
  • el conciertokon-si-er-to — hard co, soft ci
  • el disfrazdis-fras — z sounds like s
🧠 PART 3 · EL ACENTO EN -CIÓN 9 min

Words ending in -ción punch the last syllable

Every word ending in -ción carries a written accent on the ó, and the stress lands right there — on the last syllable. Don't say “can-CI-on”; say can-ci-ÓN.

  • canción → can-ci-ÓN
  • celebración → ce-le-bra-ci-ÓN

Listen for the punch at the very end:

  • la canciónstress on -ciÓN
  • la celebraciónstress on -ciÓN

Where does the stress fall in celebración? Say it, then reveal.

On the last syllable: ce-le-bra-ci-ÓN. The written accent on ó tells you exactly where to punch.
🔈 PART 4 · LA RR 5 min

The rolled rr

A double rr (and an r at the start of a word) is a trill — several quick taps of the tongue behind the top teeth. Try the trick: say the English phrase “pot of tea” fast, and feel the tt flutter. That flutter is the Spanish rr.

  • la guitarragui-TA-rra — roll the rr
  • el ritmoword-initial r also trills: RRit-mo

Don't worry if your trill isn't perfect — a light tap is understood, and it improves with practice. Aim for the flutter.

🌐 CULTURA · EL ESPAÑOL Y SUS ACENTOS
Un idioma, muchas voces

The soft-c-as-s you practiced today is called seseo, and it's how nearly all of Latin America speaks — from Mexico to Argentina. In most of Spain you'd hear a “th” sound instead (canción as “can-thi-ón”). Neither is more correct; they're regional voices of one language. The voice on these recordings uses the Latin-American seseo, the register of this course.

🎼 CULTURA A FONDO · EL ACORDEÓN Y LA CUMBIA

Listen to Colombian cumbia or Mexican norteño and one instrument leaps out: the acordeón (accordion). It arrived with German immigrants in the 1800s and was adopted so completely that today it's the soul of the sound. The Colombian city of Valledupar even hosts the Festival de la Leyenda Vallenata, where accordion players compete for days — a whole celebración built around one instrument. When you roll your rr in guitarra, remember: this music is a meeting of continents.

🎤 PART 5 · TAREA ORAL 12 min

Your graded recording (15 points)

Record yourself describing a celebration — real or imagined. Include at least two sentences in the present progressive (what people are doing right now) and at least one comparison (más/menos…que, mejor/peor, or tan…como). Aim for 30–45 seconds.

📝 A MODEL (yours should describe your own celebration)

«Estoy en una fiesta. Mis amigos están bailando y un cantante está tocando la guitarra. La música de hoy es más alegre que la del año pasado. ¡Esta celebración es la mejor!»
(Two progressive sentences — están bailando, está tocando — plus two comparisons: más…que and mejor.)

Hear the target patterns one more time before you record:

  • Están cantando…present progressive
  • Es más alegre que…a comparison
